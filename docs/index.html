02 · how a falling box can steer at all

watch both

It isn't a normal parachute.

A round parachute just falls. Wherever the air goes, it goes.

This is a parafoil — the kind skydivers land. It's a wing, so it glides forward as it descends, and pulling one side makes it turn. That is the only reason steering is possible.

It can't hover and it can't stop. Sink rate is constant, so the moment it is released its time in the air is fixed — and so is the distance it will fly. Call that budget s. The target is only d away, so it has flying to waste.

cos α = d / s Fly at angle α to the target and your path stretches by 1/cos α. Set α from that and the extra flying is spent exactly. As it descends d/s rises to 1, α falls to 0, and the weave straightens into a final approach on its own — no switching logic.

Same height, same wind, and the round one gets the same sink rate — a real one drops faster, so this flatters it.

A round canopy is a leaf in a river — it only makes drag, so it goes wherever the water goes. Twenty seconds of falling in a light breeze puts it sixty metres downwind. A parafoil is a wing, and falling is its engine: it trades height for forward speed, like a bike rolling downhill. That is what lets it move across the river instead of just floating down it.

03 · no wind sensor on board

press the buttons

It works out the wind by turning.

GPS says how fast you cross the ground. The maths says how fast you move through the air. The difference is the wind.

Flying straight, a headwind and flying slower look identical. Turning tells them apart — the wind keeps pointing one way while you swing round.

04 · the shortest way round

drag the arrow

Only six shapes exist.

It cannot turn on the spot, so it can rarely fly straight at the target.

In 1957 Lester Dubins proved the shortest path is always one of six shapes. An infinite question becomes picking the smallest of six numbers.

▶drag the blue arrow
shapelength
Grey = the losers. Pink = the one being flown.

It can only do three things: turn left in a fixed circle, turn right in a fixed circle, or go straight. Nothing in between — it cannot turn gently. So every route is those pieces stacked, and a shortest route never wastes a turn, so it is never more than three. Three pieces, two turn directions — six routes.

05 · the loop it runs

every 0.05 s

Nothing is planned in advance.

Every twentieth of a second it throws away the old plan and works out a new one from where it actually is.

So when the wind pushes it off course, there is nothing to recover — the next answer already includes it. About 800 times on the way down.

Look, guess, decide, move, drop a little height — then do it again. The wind estimate from step 2 feeds the decision in step 3, and the result of step 4 is what step 1 sees next time round.

06 · how i tested it

no cheating

I'm setting it an exam.

I make up a situation on the computer — a wind, a drop height, a target. I know all of it, because I typed it in.

But the parachute's software is never shown any of it. It only gets what real parts would give it: a GPS that wobbles, and a compass a few degrees off. Then I watch where it lands.

Give it the answer sheet and the exam means nothing.

The true wind, the exact position and the real heading stay on my side of the wall. Across it go a shaky GPS reading and a tilted compass — and nothing else. Pick a random wind, height and compass error, drop it, and repeat 150 times.

Why the last row is not a win

Row 2 looks better than row 3, but the error bars overlap, so those two are not separated at 150 drops. The honest claim is the paired one: drop for drop, working out the wind cuts the miss by 4.26 m, and wins 112 times out of 150.

What this leaves out

No gusts, no canopy flexing, no servo lag. These numbers are a best case. The model names a test build — 1 m² canopy, 0.5–0.8 kg — so a real flight can prove it wrong.

Not the first guided parafoil

The Naval Postgraduate School's Snowflake lands within 3 m, and ParaDrone already flies Dubins paths on a hobby microcontroller. No priority is claimed over either. What is new is the design analysis they do not publish.

How heavy you make it.

Too light and the wind blows it away. Too heavy and it turns too wide to fix anything at the end.

09 · the finding

Double the wind, four times the error.

To fly in wind W you need weight going up as W². And heavier means wider turns.

v = √(2mg / ρSCL)  →  v ∝ √(m/S)
R = v² / g tan φ  →  R ∝ m/S
v > W  →  (m/S)min ∝ W²
∴ Rmin ∝ W² Lift equals weight, so only the wing loading m/S sets the speed. Turn radius goes as speed squared, so it goes straight as loading. Flying in wind W needs speed above W, which needs loading as W². Compose them and the tightest turn available — the floor on landing error — grows as W². Verified to 14 decimal places: an identity of the model, not a fitted curve.

So building for wind you never meet costs accuracy even on a still day.
