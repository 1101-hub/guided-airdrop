02 · the one number that decides everything

Wing loading.

Weight divided by canopy area. It is the only thing that sets how fast the parafoil flies, because lift has to equal weight:

# parafoil.py
def airspeed(mass, area, cl):
    """V = sqrt( 2 m g / (rho S C_L) )  -- only m/S matters."""
    return np.sqrt(2.0 * mass * G / (RHO * area * cl))

Two things pull in opposite directions. Fly too slow and the wind blows you away — below the pink line the package cannot fly upwind at all, whatever the guidance says. Fly too fast and your turns get wide, so you cannot make small corrections at the end.

03 · every combination, tested

Where it lands, everywhere.

24 drops at each of 13 × 11 settings — 3,432 simulated descents. Darker means it lands further from the target. The grey wedge is where the wind beats the airspeed and no guided descent exists. Click anywhere on the chart to fly that design.

Read it like this

Go right (heavier) and the miss distance grows no matter how calm it is — wide turns. Go up (windier) and you eventually fall off the cliff into the grey.

So the best design is the lightest one that still beats the wind you expect. And because loading is set by how much you put in the box, you can retune it on the day.

04 · the shortest way round

Only six shapes exist.

The parafoil cannot turn sharper than some radius, so it usually cannot fly straight at the target. In 1957 Lester Dubins proved that the shortest path is always one of six: turn–straight–turn in four arrangements, or three arcs in two.

An infinite question — "what is the best possible curve?" — collapses into picking the smallest of six numbers. Drag the arrow to change where the package has to arrive and watch which shape wins.

05 · no wind sensor on board

How it finds the wind.

GPS says how fast you move over the ground. The glide model says how fast you move through the air. Subtract them and you have the wind.

Except the compass is biased and the airspeed is not exactly the design value — and flying in a straight line you cannot tell those apart. A 1 m/s headwind looks exactly like flying 1 m/s slower.

Turning breaks the tie. The wind keeps pointing the same way while your own airspeed swings round with you. And the parafoil already turns constantly, because that is how it burns off height. The manoeuvre that manages energy is the same manoeuvre that reveals the wind.

Being honest about the last row

The EKF row looks better than the true-wind row, but the confidence intervals overlap, so those two are not separated at 150 drops. The claim that is supported is the paired one: compared drop by drop under identical conditions, adding the EKF cuts the miss by a median 4.26 m (95% CI 3.08–6.57), and it wins in 112 of 150 trials.

What this model does not include

No turbulence, no canopy deformation, no actuator lag. So these numbers are an upper bound on how well it could do. The model names a test configuration — 1 m² canopy, 0.5–0.8 kg payload, 20 m minimum release — so a real flight can prove it wrong rather than be fitted to it.

Not the first guided parafoil

The Naval Postgraduate School's Snowflake has landed within 3 m of target, and the open-source ParaDrone already plans Dubins paths on a microcontroller. This project claims no priority over either. What it adds is the design analysis they do not publish: how wing loading and wind speed together set the floor on landing accuracy.

07 · the finding

The error floor grows with the square of the wind.

Two facts, chained together. To fly in wind W you need loading proportional to W². And turn radius grows in proportion to loading. So:

# both verified numerically, over the whole design range
R / (m/S)        = 4.7694 m³/kg   constant to 1.9e-14 %
(m/S)_min / W²   = 0.058722       constant to 1.2e-14 %

# deviations at double-precision level, so these are
# algebraic identities of the model, not fitted curves
        R_min  ∝  W²

Double the wind you design for and the smallest correction you can make at the end gets four times bigger — in every condition, including dead calm. Specifying for wind you never meet is not free insurance. It costs you accuracy all the time.
