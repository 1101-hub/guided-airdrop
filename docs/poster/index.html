A cloudburst on 19 July 2026 flooded four districts of Upper Assam. 705,000 people were affected, roads were gone, and air was the only way in.

So supplies were pushed out of helicopters. A payload released without guidance is carried by the air mass for the whole way down — it does not aim, it drifts. In this model it lands 23.9 m off. In a flood, 23.9 m is the wrong side of the water.

SO →Can you steer a falling box at all?
02 / 06

Answer: yes, if it is a wing

You can steer it.
You cannot
stop it.

A round parachute only falls. A parafoil is a ram-air canopy that glides forward as it descends, so pulling one side turns it. That forward travel is the only thing there is to aim.

v = √( 2mg / ρS CL ) How fast it flies. Only the wing loading m/S appears — mass and canopy area never act separately.

But it has no engine and no brakes. It sinks at a steady rate, so the moment it leaves the helicopter its time in the air is fixed — and with it, the total distance it will ever cover.

That distance is almost always more than the distance to the target. It cannot slow down and it cannot circle forever. It has to get rid of the surplus.

SO →It cannot fly straight at the target — it would arrive with height to spare.
03 / 06

Getting rid of the surplus

So it weaves
— on purpose.

The guidance does not fly the shortest route. It flies a route of exactly the right length, holding a track angle α off the bearing to stretch the remaining path by 1/cos α until the budget is spent.

cos α = d / s d = distance still to go  ·  s = flying left in the budget. As height runs out, d/s → 1 and α → 0 — a straight final approach, with no change of mode.

But d is not the straight line. It cannot turn on the spot, so the real distance to the target is a curve with a minimum radius. In 1957 Lester Dubins proved the shortest such curve is always one of just six shapes.

Same start (dot) and target (cross), same heading at each end, solved six ways at one shared scale — computed by the model, not drawn. All six closed forms agree with step-wise integration to 9.0 × 10−12 m over 3,514 solutions.

So it checks all six, takes the shortest, and measures its budget against that. Dubins measures; the weave spends.

SO →From release to landing, it is almost always turning.
04 / 06

The part that pays for itself

And that is
how it finds
the wind.

Nothing on board measures the wind and nothing is sent from the ground. An Extended Kalman Filter works it out from satellite velocity alone — because the wind is a subtraction:

State carried: [ wx, wy, va, b ] — wind east and north, airspeed, and magnetometer bias. The bias enters through the heading trigonometry, which is what makes the problem non-linear.

Except it should be impossible

On a fixed heading, a headwind and an airspeed that is simply too low produce exactly the same track. No amount of data separates them — the state is unobservable. Flying straight, this cannot work at all.

FLYING STRAIGHT

Headwind, or an airspeed that is too low? Same track. Unsolvable.

TURNING

The two predict different tracks. The filter can tell them apart.

And by panel 03 it is already turning, for reasons that had nothing whatever to do with the wind. The manoeuvre that makes the wind measurable is one it was committed to anyway. The measurement is free.

SO →Build the most capable canopy you can afford?
06 / 06

The one number that decides everything

No. A heavier
one is worse.

To make headway against wind of speed W it must fly faster than W, which forces wing loading up as W2. But turn radius rises linearly with loading — and turn radius is the smallest correction it can still make in the final seconds.

Rmin ∝ W2 The two requirements fight each other. The error floor follows the wind you designed for, not the wind you actually meet.

So building for a gale you never encounter makes you less accurate on a calm day. The right choice is the lowest loading that still penetrates, with margin — not the most capable one.

And loading is set by ballast. Unlike canopy area, it can be matched to the forecast on the morning of the drop.

Every combination of wing loading and wind speed, 24 drops each. The dark valley is the right answer, and it moves as the wind does.

  2. Lay all six out on the backing sheet before gluing anything. Line up the tops of panels 01–03, then 04–06.

  4. Scan both QR codes with your own phone once the poster is up. Test under the lighting in the hall, not at home.

If a QR code will not scan

Both codes were decoded from this very PDF at every resolution from draft to 300 dpi, so the artwork is not the problem. In practice it is nearly always glare — a reflection off tape, glue or a glossy sheet, sitting across the middle of the code. Move so the light is behind you rather than behind the phone. Failing that, low ink leaves gaps in the modules: reprint that one sheet. The codes carry the highest error correction level, so a crease or a fingerprint will not stop them.

Reading order

01 sets up the problem and sends them to the first scan. 02–04 are the model: why it can steer, how it finds the wind, how far it really has to fly. 05 is the evidence, including what it does not show. 06 is the finding, and sends them to the second scan — so a judge who only reads the top row still gets the problem, the mechanism and a demonstration.
