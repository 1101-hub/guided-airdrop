Part one · presenting

The spine

Six sentences. If you are interrupted, cut anything but these.

  1. Supplies dropped by helicopter drift with the wind and land far off.
  2. A parafoil glides, so it can be steered.
  3. It cannot stop, so it always has spare distance to burn — that is why it weaves.
  4. Weaving is what makes the wind measurable with no wind sensor.
  5. That cuts the miss from 23.9 m to 2.35 m.
  6. And the surprise: a heavier canopy is worse, because it turns wider.
The one-sentence version

“It works out the wind on the way down without carrying a wind sensor — and the manoeuvre that lets it do that is one it had to make anyway.”

At the table

Assume four minutes. You will often get two.

0:00
Hand them your phone with scan 1 already open. Do not explain first. Let them press drop it and watch it land in the water. Say only: “that is a supply drop with no idea what the wind is doing.”
0:30
Name the problem. Assam, July 2026: 705,000 affected, 80,000 unreached, roads gone. Point at panel 01. Do not linger — they can read.
1:00
Panels 02 and 03 — the mechanism. A wing, not a dome, so it glides and can be steered. But it cannot stop, so it has surplus distance. Point at the hatched bar. “That surplus is why it weaves.”
1:45
Panel 04 — the turn. This is the part to slow down on. On a fixed heading a headwind and a slow airspeed look identical. Turning separates them. And it was turning anyway. The measurement is free.
2:30

Two rules

Never read the poster aloud. They can read faster than you can talk; your job is the connective tissue between panels.

Hand over the phone rather than showing them. A judge who presses the button remembers it. A judge who watches you press it does not.

The page, screen by screen

Nine sections. You will never show all nine. Each entry gives the one thing to press and the one thing to say while it runs.

01 · try it yourself

Press: leave every slider alone and press drop it. Then change one thing and drop again — the line under the readout tells them whether it helped.

Say: “Every section on this page is the same model. These sliders feed the same code the paper ran 150 times.”

Your best single move in the whole talk: set weight to 0.25, drop, then drag it to 2.5 and drop again. Nothing else changes. That is the finding in ten seconds.

Two buttons worth knowing: race all three flies all three wind-knowledge settings at once, and 50 drops at once is your answer to “was that just luck?”

Trap

Never defend a single drop. One drop is one sample. If a judge lands a bad one, press 50 drops at once and talk about the spread instead.

02 · how a falling box can steer at all

Press: drop both. A round canopy and a parafoil fall together.

Say: “The round one goes wherever the air goes. The parafoil trades height for distance — and that forward travel is the only thing there is to aim.”

Volunteer this: the round canopy is given the same sink rate, which flatters it — a real one drops faster still. Saying that before they spot it is worth more than the demo.

03 · no wind sensor on board

Press force it straight first. Let them watch the wind estimate fail to settle. Then press let it turn.

Say: “Flying straight, a headwind and an airspeed that is too low give exactly the same track. The filter cannot tell them apart — not because it is bad, but because the information is not there. Turning changes the geometry and separates them.”

The number to quote if pushed: over 150 drops, forced straight the wind error is 3.39 m/s; free to turn it is 0.07 m/s.

Slow down here

This is the cleverest thing in the project. It is also the only part a judge cannot get from the poster alone, because it needs the before and after. Give it twice the time you give any other section.

04 · the shortest way round

Drag the blue arrow — it sets the heading you must arrive on. Keep dragging until the winning shape changes.

Say: “Six shapes, and which one wins keeps changing as I drag. That is why it evaluates all six every step rather than guessing one.”

If they ask why the end heading matters: it is landing, not stopping. Arriving over the target pointing the wrong way is not an arrival.

05 · the result

Press: nothing. It is a table — let them read it.

Say: “Same conditions in every row. The only thing that changes is what the guidance knows about the wind.”

Trap

Row 2 looks better than row 3. Do not claim it. Point at the overlap note yourself: “those two intervals overlap, so I can't separate them at 150 drops. What I can defend is row 1 against row 2.” Saying this first is the single most credible thing you can do.

06 · the one number that decides everything

Drag the weight slider slowly, end to end.

Say: “Too light and it cannot fly into the wind at all. Too heavy and its turns get too wide to fix anything at the end. The best answer is somewhere in between, and it moves when the wind does.”

07 · the finding

Press: nothing. This is the one to talk over.

Say: “Double the wind and you need four times the weight to fly in it — and the turn radius follows. Double the wind, four times the error floor.”

If they want the exact statement, this is where you give it: Rmin = W²/(g tanφ) — and note that every canopy parameter cancels, so the floor is the same whatever wing you pick. See the floor.

08 · every combination, tested

Click a square inside the dark valley, and watch section 01 fly that design. Then click one far outside it and let it fly that.

Say: “3,432 drops on one picture. Dark is close, grey cannot fly at all. The valley is the right answer, and it drifts as the wind rises — that drift is the W² law.”

Links to the paper. Use it only if a judge asks for the write-up, or if you want to end on “it is all there, with the limitations listed.”

Part one · presenting

Three kinds of judge

The one in a hurry

Give them scan 1, the weight slider twice, and sentence six. Ninety seconds. Do not start at the beginning.

The one who wants to poke holes

Get there first. Say the limitations before they ask: no turbulence, no canopy flexing, no servo lag, so these numbers are a best case. Then say the model names a test build — 1 m² canopy, 0.5–0.8 kg, 20 m minimum release — so a real flight can prove it wrong. Volunteering the weakness is what makes the rest credible.

The one who knows the field

They will ask what is new, because guided parafoils exist. Answer straight: the Naval Postgraduate School lands one within 3 m, ParaDrone flies Dubins paths on a hobby microcontroller, and you claim no priority over either. What neither publishes is the design analysis — how wing loading and wind together set the floor on accuracy. That part is yours.

When something goes wrong

  • A QR code will not scan. Almost always glare. Move so the light is behind you. Failing that, the page is at 1101-hub.github.io/guided-airdrop.
  • The AR will not start on their phone. It needs camera permission, and on an iPhone motion permission as well. If it refuses, open the ordinary page instead — it makes the same point in 2D.
  • The miss looks bad on screen. Never defend one drop. Press 50 drops at once and talk about the distribution. See the answer sheet.
  • You lose your thread. Go to the weight slider. It works from anywhere in the talk and it is the finding.

Part two · the maths

Steady glide

In a steady glide nothing accelerates, so the forces balance along and across the flight path. Lift carries the component of weight perpendicular to the path, drag carries the component along it.

L = W cos γ    D = W sin γ W = mg is the weight, γ the glide angle below horizontal.

Dividing one by the other gives the glide angle from the aerodynamics alone. Nothing about mass or size survives:

tan γ = D / L = CD / CL = 1 / E E = CL/CD is the glide ratio. With CL = 0.80 and CD = 0.27, E = 2.96 and γ = 18.6°.

Writing lift as L = ½ρV²SCL and setting it equal to W cosγ gives the speed:

V = √( 2mg cosγ / ρS CL ) Then vh = V cosγ across the ground and vz = V sinγ downwards. Sink rate is constant, which is the fact everything else rests on.
Expect

“Why does a heavier one fly faster?” — Because lift must match weight. Lift rises with the square of speed, so doubling the weight needs √2 times the speed. It does not fall faster relative to its path; it flies the same angle, faster along it.

Part two · the maths

Wing loading is the only number

In the speed equation, mass and area never appear apart — only the ratio m/S, the wing loading. A 2 kg payload under 2 m² flies exactly like a 1 kg payload under 1 m².

vh = k √( m/S )    where k = cosγ √( 2g cosγ / ρCL ) k collects everything that is not the loading.

This is why the design chart on panel 06 has wing loading on an axis rather than mass and area separately. It collapses two design choices into one number, which is the whole reason the chart is two-dimensional and not three.

Part two · the maths

The turn

To turn, the canopy banks. Lift tilts with it, so the vertical component still holds the weight up while the horizontal component pulls it round the corner — that horizontal component is the centripetal force.

L sinφ = mv²/R     L cosφ = mg φ is the bank angle. Divide the first by the second.
R = vh² / ( g tanφ ) Mass cancels. The model uses φ = 20°.

Now compose it with the previous section. Since vh² = k²(m/S):

R = k² (m/S) / ( g tanφ )   ⇒   R ∝ m/S Turn radius is exactly linear in wing loading. Double the weight per square metre, double the turning circle.

Check it on the page: at 1.20 kg/m² the readout says a 5.7 m tightest turn; at 2.40 it says 11.4 m. Exactly double.

Part two · the maths · the strongest thing you have

The floor

To make any headway against wind of speed W, the canopy has to fly faster than the wind. Otherwise it is blown backwards whatever it does:

vh > W   ⇒   k√(m/S) > W   ⇒   (m/S)min = W² / k² The minimum usable wing loading rises with the square of the wind.

Substitute that minimum loading back into the turn radius. The k² cancels:

Rmin = W² / ( g tanφ ) The tightest turn available to any canopy that can still fly in wind W.

Every canopy parameter has vanished. Lift coefficient, drag coefficient, air density, mass, area — none of them appear. They decide how heavy you must build it, but not the manoeuvring floor you end up with.

Say it like this

“If it has to fly in a 3 m/s wind, the tightest turn it can possibly make is 2.5 m across — and that is true whatever canopy you pick. The design decides how heavy you must be, not how well you can manoeuvre once you are.”

Expect

“So why not just bank harder?” — Fair. The floor scales as 1/tanφ, so a steeper bank tightens it. But bank costs lift, so it increases sink rate and shortens the flight. The model fixes φ = 20°; treating bank as a design variable is the obvious next piece of work.

Part two · the maths

Dubins paths

The canopy cannot turn on the spot. It has a minimum radius R, so the real distance between two points is never the straight line — it is a curve.

In 1957 Lester Dubins proved that for a vehicle moving forward at constant speed with a lower bound on turning radius, the shortest path between two oriented positions — a place and a heading at each end — is always at most three segments, each either a maximum-rate turn (L or R) or a straight run (S), in one of six orderings:

LSL   LSR   RSL   RSR   RLR   LRL Four with a straight middle, two that are all turns. The CCC pair only exists when the endpoints are close together.

This turns an infinite search into arithmetic. Each of the six has a closed form, so you evaluate all six and take the smallest. The model does that at every step of the descent, and the six closed forms were checked against step-wise integration of the same motion — largest disagreement 9.0 × 10⁻¹² m over 3,514 solutions, which is floating-point noise, not error.

Expect

“Why do you need the heading at the end?” — Because it is landing, not stopping. Arriving over the target pointing the wrong way is not an arrival. Fixing both endpoints and both headings is exactly the problem Dubins solved.

Part two · the maths

Spending the budget

Sink rate is constant, so from a height h the time aloft is fixed, and so is the total distance it will ever fly:

t = h / vz     s = vh t s is the path budget: how much flying is left.

The target is usually nearer than that. The surplus cannot be saved, so the guidance lengthens the path deliberately: hold a track angle α off the bearing to the target, and the path stretches by 1/cosα. Setting the stretched path equal to the budget gives the control law:

cos α = d / s As height runs out, d/s → 1 and α → 0 — the weave straightens into a final approach on its own, with no mode switch.

The subtle part: d is not the straight-line distance. It is the Dubins length to the target, because that is the distance it will actually have to fly. Using the straight line makes the vehicle believe it has spare height it does not have, and it arrives short. That is the line in the code reading dEff = turnPath.length.

The sentence that ties it together

“Dubins measures; the weave spends.”

Part two · the maths

The filter

Nothing on board measures the wind. But satellites give velocity over the ground, and the flight model gives velocity through the air. The wind is the difference — a vector subtraction.

The problem is that the airspeed and the heading are not known exactly either, so you cannot just subtract once. An Extended Kalman Filter estimates four unknowns at once:

x = [ wx, wy, va, b ] wind east and north, true airspeed, and the compass bias.

Predicted ground velocity from that state, using the measured heading θm corrected by the bias:

h(x) = [ va cos(θm+b) + wx , va sin(θm+b) + wy ]

The bias sits inside a cosine, so this is not linear in the state — which is exactly why an ordinary Kalman filter will not do and the extended one is needed. The EKF linearises at the current estimate by taking the Jacobian:

H = [ 1  0  cosθ  −vasinθ ;  0  1  sinθ  vacosθ ] θ = θm + b. Then the standard predict/update: innovation, Kalman gain, covariance.

Kalman's 1960 method is the optimal way to combine a prediction with a noisy measurement when both have known uncertainty. Each GNSS reading nudges all four estimates, weighted by how confident the filter currently is in each.

Expect

“Why estimate the compass error too?” — Because it is indistinguishable from wind if you do not. A compass reading 10° off makes the vehicle mis-attribute its own drift. Estimating it jointly is also why the filter is not clearly beaten by simply being told the true wind: being told the wind leaves the compass error uncorrected.

Part two · the maths · the cleverest part

Observability

Here is the trap. On a fixed heading, the filter cannot work at all — and no amount of extra data fixes it.

Look at the Jacobian. The airspeed column is (cosθ, sinθ) and the wind columns are (1,0) and (0,1). If θ never changes, those directions never change either. You are measuring a 2-vector to determine 4 unknowns, and the same two combinations stay invisible forever:

va + δ   with   wind − δ(cosθ, sinθ) produces an identical ground velocity. A headwind and an airspeed that is too low are the same measurement.

The state is unobservable. Turning changes θ, so those columns rotate, and readings taken at different headings constrain different combinations. Stack enough of them and the ambiguity closes.

Measured over 150 drops at the page's default settings: forced straight, the median wind error is 3.39 m/s — the filter is guessing. Free to turn, it is 0.07 m/s. A factor of forty-six, from nothing but being allowed to change heading.

Why this is the good bit

Turning is normally a cost you pay for information. Here the vehicle had to turn anyway, to burn off surplus distance. The manoeuvre that makes the wind observable is one the guidance was already committed to. The measurement is free.

Part two · the maths

The statistics

Why the median, not the mean

Miss distance is heavy-tailed: most drops land close, a few land very far. One bad drop drags a mean around. The median says what a typical drop does, which is the question being asked. The 90th percentile is reported alongside so the bad tail is not hidden.

Bootstrap confidence intervals

There is no tidy formula for the uncertainty on a median. So: resample the 150 results with replacement, 20,000 times, take the median of each resample, and report the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of those 20,000 medians. That interval is where the true median plausibly sits.

Paired comparison

Every configuration flies the same 150 sets of conditions — same wind, same height, same seed. So each drop can be compared with itself. That removes the variation between conditions and leaves only the effect of the guidance. It is why the paired reduction of 4.26 m is a stronger statement than comparing two medians.

“Your method beat being told the answer?” — Do not claim that. Those two intervals overlap across most of their range, so at this sample size they are not separated. Say: “I can't separate those two — I'd need more drops. What I can defend is that working it out beats not knowing, and that is a factor of two.” On the page's default settings, being told the wind is clearly better. Know that before a judge finds it.

Part two · the maths

What it cannot do

Say these before you are asked.

  • No turbulence. The wind field is uniform and steady. Real air gusts, so the accuracy reported is an upper bound.
  • No canopy deformation. CL and CD are fixed. A real canopy flexes and its coefficients move.
  • No actuator dynamics. Turns begin instantly. Real servos take time, which costs accuracy in the final seconds — exactly where it matters most.
  • Simulation, not flight. Nothing here has been dropped.

The honest framing: the model commits to numbers first and names a test build — 1 m² canopy, 0.5–0.8 kg payload, 20 m minimum release — so a real flight can prove it wrong rather than be fitted to it. That is the difference between a prediction and a description.
